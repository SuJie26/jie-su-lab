Connecting People and Nature for Coastal Sustainability

Coastal ecosystems support biodiversity, livelihoods, and a wide range of economic activities. Their future depends on how conservation, restoration, and resource use are managed, particularly as climate change creates new pressures and uncertainties.

Our research brings together social–ecological systems science and ecological economics to understand these connections and inform coastal management. We combine global evidence synthesis with local field research to examine how coastal systems change, assess the benefits they provide, and identify effective conservation and restoration strategies. Across these areas, we connect biodiversity conservation, climate mitigation and adaptation, economic development, and human well-being.

Understanding coastal social–ecological dynamics

We study how coastal ecosystems and human societies shape one another over time. This includes examining how land use, economic activities, and management policies affect coastal environments, and how ecological changes influence livelihoods and development. We seek to understand why coastal regions follow different trajectories and what enables them to withstand disturbance and recover.

At the global scale, our research has quantified the ecological and economic outcomes of mangrove restoration and identified factors that influence its effectiveness. In Xiamen Bay, we combine spatial analysis, surveys, and choice experiments to examine ecological suitability, ecosystem service values, and public preferences. These studies help align restoration planning with local environmental conditions and social priorities.

Building on this work, we are developing long-term ecological and socioeconomic datasets across coastal settings to investigate the drivers of change, stability, and recovery.

Assessing ecosystem benefits and sustainable use

Understanding coastal change also requires examining how people benefit from ecosystems and how management decisions affect those benefits. We combine ecological monitoring, ecosystem service valuation, cost-benefit analysis, and social surveys to evaluate conservation, restoration, and resource-use options. We consider both trade-offs among ecological, economic, and social outcomes and the distribution of costs and benefits across different groups.

Our projects supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China focus on integrated mangrove–aquaculture systems, coastal wetland ecotourism, and restoration under different management objectives. They examine sustainability outcomes, the conditions needed for implementation, and how ecological and landscape features shape visitor and resident experiences. Together, these studies explore how conservation, restoration, and sustainable use can support regional development.

Informing conservation and restoration planning

We use insights into coastal dynamics and ecosystem benefits to inform decisions about where to act, which measures to use, and how management can respond to changing conditions.

Our spatial planning research identifies priority areas for mixed-species mangrove restoration by integrating ecological suitability, species diversity, landscape connectivity, and restoration costs. We also evaluate trade-offs between alternative coastal policies to help align conservation and restoration with local priorities.

Future research will combine climate and socioeconomic scenarios to assess alternative conservation, restoration, and resource-use strategies. By examining their long-term benefits, risks, and feasibility, we aim to help coastal communities and decision-makers plan for sustainable development under uncertainty.

Developing nature-based solutions for coastal sustainability

We investigate how protecting, restoring, and sustainably managing coastal ecosystems can address societal challenges while supporting biodiversity and human well-being. We focus on their contribution to climate mitigation and adaptation, sustainable livelihoods, and coastal development.

We assess the ecological and economic outcomes of mangrove restoration and compare engineered, ecosystem-based, and hybrid coastal defence measures. This work helps evaluate the effectiveness of nature-based approaches, their benefits and trade-offs, and how they can complement engineering.

Our wider work on climate adaptation across aquaculture value chains has identified 53 adaptation options and the policy, information, and financial barriers affecting their implementation. Building on this evidence, we aim to identify the conditions that enable nature-based solutions to be adopted and sustained in coastal settings.
